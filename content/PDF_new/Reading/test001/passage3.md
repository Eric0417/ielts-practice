# The Decline of Insect Populations

Insects constitute the most diverse and abundant group of animals on Earth, representing approximately 80% of all known animal species and playing indispensable roles in virtually every terrestrial and freshwater ecosystem. They pollinate plants, decompose organic matter, control pest populations, and serve as a critical food source for countless birds, amphibians, reptiles, and mammals. Yet in recent decades, a growing body of scientific evidence has documented alarming declines in insect populations across the globe, a phenomenon that some researchers have described as an impending 'insect apocalypse.'

The scale of the decline is staggering. A comprehensive review published in 2019 synthesised data from 73 historical reports and concluded that over 40% of insect species are threatened with extinction, with total insect biomass declining at a rate of approximately 2.5% per year. In some regions, the losses have been even more dramatic. Studies conducted in German nature reserves documented a 75% decline in flying insect biomass over a 27-year period, while research in Puerto Rico's rainforests recorded biomass losses of up to 98% over 35 years.

Habitat loss driven by agricultural intensification is widely considered the primary driver of insect decline. The conversion of diverse natural landscapes into monoculture croplands, combined with the removal of hedgerows, field margins, and other semi-natural habitats, has dramatically reduced the available food sources and breeding sites for insects. Urbanisation has further fragmented and degraded insect habitats, creating barriers to dispersal and isolating populations.

Chemical pollution, particularly from agricultural pesticides, represents another major threat. Neonicotinoid pesticides, which are systemic compounds absorbed by all plant tissues including pollen and nectar, have been strongly implicated in the decline of pollinator populations. These neurotoxic chemicals impair navigation, foraging behaviour, and reproductive success in bees and other beneficial insects even at sub-lethal doses. While the European Union has imposed severe restrictions on neonicotinoids, their use continues in many other parts of the world.

Climate change further compounds these pressures. Rising temperatures are altering the phenology — the timing of life cycle events — of both insects and the plants they depend on. When the emergence of adult insects becomes desynchronised from the flowering of their host plants, a phenological mismatch occurs that can disrupt entire food webs. Extreme weather events, including droughts and heatwaves, can cause direct mortality among insect populations.

The consequences of insect decline extend far beyond the insects themselves. Approximately 35% of global food crops depend to some degree on insect pollination, with an estimated economic value of 235 to 577 billion US dollars annually. Declines in insectivorous bird populations have been directly linked to reductions in insect prey availability. The loss of decomposer insects such as dung beetles and carrion beetles slows nutrient cycling and can lead to the accumulation of organic waste. Furthermore, the reduction of natural enemies of crop pests — predatory and parasitic insects that provide biological control services — may increase reliance on chemical pesticides, creating a vicious cycle.
