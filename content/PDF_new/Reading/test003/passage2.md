# Antibiotic Resistance: A Growing Global Crisis

The discovery of penicillin by Alexander Fleming in 1928 and its subsequent mass production during World War II ushered in what has been called the golden age of antibiotics. For the first time in human history, common bacterial infections that had been leading causes of death — pneumonia, tuberculosis, sepsis, and wound infections — could be effectively treated. The impact on human health was nothing short of revolutionary. Global life expectancy increased dramatically, and surgical procedures that had previously been too risky due to infection became routine.

However, even as Fleming accepted his Nobel Prize in 1945, he warned of a looming threat. In his acceptance speech, he described a scenario in which a patient self-medicates with insufficient doses of penicillin, allowing the bacteria to survive and develop resistance. This warning proved prescient. Today, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most serious global public health threats of the 21st century, with the World Health Organization declaring it one of the top ten threats to global health.

Antibiotic resistance is a natural evolutionary phenomenon accelerated by human behaviour. Bacteria reproduce rapidly, with generation times as short as 20 minutes, and their high mutation rates mean that random genetic changes conferring resistance can arise frequently. When antibiotics are used, whether appropriately or not, they create a powerful selective pressure — susceptible bacteria are killed, while those with resistance mutations survive and proliferate. The misuse and overuse of antibiotics in both human medicine and agriculture have dramatically accelerated this process.

In many parts of the world, antibiotics are available over the counter without a prescription, leading to widespread self-medication for conditions for which antibiotics are ineffective, such as viral infections. Even when prescribed appropriately, patients frequently fail to complete the full course of treatment, providing an opportunity for partially resistant bacteria to survive and replicate. In agriculture, approximately 70% of medically important antibiotics sold globally are used in livestock, primarily for growth promotion and disease prevention in crowded conditions, rather than for treating sick animals.

The consequences of widespread antibiotic resistance are already being felt. The emergence of multi-drug resistant tuberculosis, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae has made common infections increasingly difficult and expensive to treat. Surgical procedures, cancer chemotherapy, and organ transplantation — all of which rely on effective antibiotics to prevent and treat infections — are becoming riskier. The United Nations estimates that without urgent action, AMR could cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050, surpassing cancer as a cause of death.

Addressing AMR requires a multifaceted approach. Stewardship programmes in hospitals and clinics aim to ensure that antibiotics are prescribed only when necessary and that the correct drug, dose, and duration are used. Development of new antibiotics has been slow, largely because the economic incentives for pharmaceutical companies are insufficient — new antibiotics must be used sparingly to preserve their effectiveness, which limits their profitability. Alternative approaches, including bacteriophage therapy, antimicrobial peptides, and vaccines against resistant pathogens, are being actively researched but remain largely experimental.
