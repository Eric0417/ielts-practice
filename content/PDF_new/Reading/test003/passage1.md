# The Silk Road: Trade Routes That Changed the World

The Silk Road was not a single road but a vast network of trade routes spanning over 6,400 kilometres across Asia, connecting China with the Mediterranean world. For more than 1,500 years, from approximately 130 BCE to the 15th century CE, these routes facilitated the exchange of goods, technologies, religions, artistic styles, and even diseases between the great civilisations of the East and West.

The name 'Silk Road' is somewhat misleading, first coined in 1877 by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen. While silk from China was indeed one of the most valuable commodities transported along these routes, it was far from the only one. Spices from India and Southeast Asia, glassware from Rome, ceramics and paper from China, horses from Central Asia, and precious stones from Afghanistan all travelled these routes. Perhaps more importantly, the Silk Road served as a conduit for the transmission of ideas — Buddhism spread from India to China and Japan; Islam reached Central Asia and beyond; and technologies including papermaking, gunpowder, and the magnetic compass travelled westward, eventually transforming European society.

The geography of the Silk Road presented formidable challenges to travellers and merchants. The routes traversed some of the world's most inhospitable terrain — the Taklamakan Desert, known in local languages as the 'desert of death'; the Pamir Mountains, often called the 'roof of the world'; and the vast, arid steppes of Central Asia. Caravans of Bactrian camels, uniquely adapted to extreme temperatures and capable of carrying loads of up to 200 kilograms, were essential to the functioning of these trade routes.

A distinctive feature of Silk Road trade was that goods rarely travelled the entire route with a single merchant. Instead, trade operated through a relay system, with goods changing hands multiple times at oasis cities that served as commercial hubs. Cities such as Samarkand, Bukhara, and Kashgar grew wealthy as entrepôts, where merchants from different regions could exchange goods without undertaking the entire perilous journey. This segmented trade structure meant that cultural and technological exchange occurred through a series of localised interactions rather than through direct contact between the empires at either end of the route.

The decline of the Silk Road began in the 15th century, driven by several converging factors. The collapse of the Mongol Empire, which had provided the political stability and security necessary for overland trade, led to increased banditry and the fragmentation of the trade routes. More decisively, the development of maritime navigation technologies enabled European powers to establish direct sea routes to Asia, bypassing the overland routes entirely. The Portuguese discovery of the Cape of Good Hope route to India in 1498 marked a turning point, as oceanic shipping proved faster, cheaper, and capable of carrying far larger cargoes than camel caravans.

Today, the Silk Road has captured the modern imagination once again. China's ambitious Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, explicitly invokes the historical Silk Road as a model for massive infrastructure investments connecting Asia, Africa, and Europe. This modern incarnation, involving railways, ports, pipelines, and digital connectivity, represents one of the largest infrastructure projects in human history.
