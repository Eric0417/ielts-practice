# The Global Plastic Pollution Crisis

Plastics have transformed modern life since their widespread introduction in the mid-20th century. Lightweight, durable, versatile, and cheap to produce, plastics have found applications in virtually every sector of the economy, from packaging and construction to medicine and transportation. Annual global plastic production has surged from 2 million tonnes in 1950 to over 400 million tonnes today, and this figure is projected to double again by 2040 if current trends continue.

The very properties that make plastics so useful — their durability and resistance to degradation — also make them an environmental nightmare. Unlike organic materials, most plastics do not biodegrade on any meaningful human timescale. Instead, they break down into increasingly smaller fragments through mechanical weathering and photodegradation, eventually forming microplastics — particles smaller than 5 millimetres in diameter — and nanoplastics, which are measured in billionths of a metre.

The scale of plastic pollution entering the world's oceans is staggering. An estimated 11 million metric tonnes of plastic waste flow into the oceans each year, equivalent to dumping a garbage truck of plastic into the sea every minute. This figure is projected to nearly triple by 2040. Once in the marine environment, plastics have been found in every corner of the globe, from the deepest ocean trenches to Arctic sea ice. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch, a vast accumulation of floating debris located between Hawaii and California, covers an estimated area of 1.6 million square kilometres — roughly three times the size of France.

The ecological impacts of plastic pollution are wide-ranging and severe. Over 900 marine species are known to be affected through entanglement, ingestion, or habitat damage. Seabirds mistake floating plastic fragments for food and feed them to their chicks, often with fatal consequences. Sea turtles confuse plastic bags with jellyfish, their natural prey. Microplastics have been found in the digestive systems of organisms at every level of the marine food web, from zooplankton to whales.

More recently, concern has shifted to the potential human health impacts of plastic pollution. Microplastics have been detected in drinking water, seafood, table salt, and even the air we breathe. A 2019 study commissioned by the World Wildlife Fund estimated that the average person may consume up to 5 grams of plastic per week — the equivalent of a credit card. While the health effects of chronic microplastic ingestion remain poorly understood, research has shown that plastics can act as vectors for toxic chemicals, absorbing persistent organic pollutants from the environment and releasing potentially harmful additives such as bisphenols and phthalates.

Efforts to address the plastic crisis have gained momentum in recent years. Over 60 countries have introduced bans or restrictions on single-use plastics, with varying degrees of enforcement and success. The United Nations Environment Assembly adopted a resolution in 2022 to negotiate a legally binding global treaty on plastic pollution by 2024. However, experts emphasise that recycling alone cannot solve the problem — only about 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled. A systemic approach combining reduced production, improved waste management infrastructure, circular economy principles, and innovation in biodegradable materials will be essential to address the scale of the challenge.
