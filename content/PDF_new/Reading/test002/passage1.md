# The Dawn of Written Language

The invention of writing represents one of the most transformative developments in human history, marking the transition from prehistory to recorded history and fundamentally altering the capacity of societies to accumulate, preserve, and transmit knowledge across generations. While spoken language likely emerged tens of thousands of years ago, the earliest known writing systems date back only about 5,500 years, arising independently in at least four locations: Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, and China.

The Sumerians of ancient Mesopotamia are generally credited with developing the first true writing system around 3400 BCE. This system, known as cuneiform, began as a series of pictographic symbols pressed into wet clay tablets using a wedge-shaped stylus. Initially used for administrative record-keeping — tracking agricultural yields, trade transactions, and tax obligations — cuneiform gradually evolved to represent not only objects but also sounds and abstract concepts. By 2600 BCE, cuneiform had become sophisticated enough to record literature, legal codes, and religious texts, including the famous Epic of Gilgamesh.

In Egypt, hieroglyphic writing emerged around 3200 BCE, roughly contemporaneous with cuneiform. Egyptian hieroglyphs were a complex system combining logographic, syllabic, and alphabetic elements, with over 700 distinct symbols. Hieroglyphs were used primarily for monumental inscriptions and religious texts, while a simplified cursive form called hieratic was employed for everyday administrative and literary purposes. The decipherment of hieroglyphs in the early 19th century by Jean-François Champollion, using the Rosetta Stone as a key, opened up a vast corpus of Egyptian literature and historical records to modern scholarship.

The Indus Valley civilisation, which flourished in what is now Pakistan and northwest India, developed its own writing system around 2600 BCE. Known as the Indus script, it appears on seals, pottery, and other artefacts. Despite decades of scholarly effort, the Indus script remains undeciphered, largely because the inscriptions are typically very short — averaging only five characters — and no bilingual text comparable to the Rosetta Stone has been discovered. The inability to read the Indus script severely limits our understanding of this sophisticated urban civilisation.

In China, the earliest evidence of writing comes from oracle bones dating to the Shang Dynasty, around 1200 BCE. These inscriptions, carved into turtle shells and animal bones, were used for divination rituals. The Chinese writing system is unique among the world's major scripts in that it has maintained a continuous tradition of use for over 3,000 years. Modern Chinese characters are direct descendants of these ancient forms, although the system has undergone considerable simplification and standardisation.

The development of alphabetic writing marked another major milestone. The first widely used alphabet was developed by the Phoenicians around 1050 BCE. Unlike earlier systems that required hundreds of symbols, the Phoenician alphabet used just 22 characters representing consonant sounds. This innovation dramatically reduced the complexity of literacy, making writing accessible to a much broader segment of society. The Phoenician alphabet was subsequently adapted by the Greeks, who added symbols for vowels, and then by the Romans, whose Latin alphabet became the most widely used writing system in the world.
