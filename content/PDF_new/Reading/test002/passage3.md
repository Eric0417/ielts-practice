# Why We Sleep: The Neuroscience of Rest

Sleep is a biological necessity that consumes roughly one-third of human life, yet for centuries its fundamental purpose remained one of biology's most persistent mysteries. In recent decades, however, neuroscientific research has dramatically advanced our understanding of what happens in the brain during sleep and why it is so essential for physical and cognitive health.

Sleep is not a single uniform state but a dynamic process consisting of distinct stages that cycle throughout the night. Non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, which dominates the early part of the night, is characterised by slow, synchronised brain waves and is divided into three stages of increasing depth. Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, in contrast, features brain activity patterns remarkably similar to wakefulness, along with the characteristic rapid eye movements and temporary muscle paralysis that prevents us from physically acting out our dreams. A typical night's sleep involves four to six of these NREM-REM cycles, each lasting approximately 90 minutes.

One of the most important functions of sleep is memory consolidation. During NREM sleep, particularly deep slow-wave sleep, the brain actively transfers memories from the hippocampus — a temporary storage site — to the neocortex for long-term storage. This process, known as systems consolidation, not only preserves memories but also integrates them with existing knowledge networks. REM sleep, meanwhile, appears to play a specialised role in emotional memory processing and creative problem-solving, which may explain why people often wake with novel insights to problems they contemplated before sleep.

The glymphatic system, discovered only in 2012, represents another critical function of sleep. During deep sleep, cerebrospinal fluid flows through the brain in rhythmic pulses, flushing out metabolic waste products that accumulate during waking hours. Among these waste products is beta-amyloid, a protein that forms the characteristic plaques found in the brains of Alzheimer's disease patients. This finding has established a compelling link between chronic sleep deprivation and increased risk of neurodegenerative disease.

Sleep also plays a crucial role in emotional regulation. Sleep deprivation, even for a single night, produces a 60% amplification in the reactivity of the amygdala to negative emotional stimuli. Simultaneously, the connection between the amygdala and the medial prefrontal cortex — which normally provides top-down emotional regulation — is weakened. This neurological double blow helps explain why insufficient sleep is so strongly associated with mood disorders, anxiety, and impaired social functioning.

Despite the overwhelming evidence of sleep's importance, modern society faces what researchers have termed a 'sleep loss epidemic.' Artificial lighting, screen technology, shift work, and the cultural valorisation of busyness have all contributed to widespread sleep restriction. The World Health Organization has classified night shift work as a probable carcinogen, based on the disruption of circadian rhythms, and evidence increasingly suggests that chronic sleep deprivation contributes to cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes, and impaired immune function.
